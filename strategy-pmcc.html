Strategy Explained

Construction: buy a deep in-the-money call at the furthest available expiry (a high-delta call stands in for holding the underlying), then sell a near-dated out-of-the-money call against it — the same two-expiry, two-strike shape as the Diagonal Spread page, but with strikes deliberately chosen at the far ends (deep ITM / OTM) rather than near-ATM.

Market view: bullish-to-neutral, same as a real covered call — willing to cap upside above the short strike in exchange for income, but wants the long leg to track BTC's price closely.

Why trade it: the deep ITM call costs a fraction of spot (mostly intrinsic value plus a smaller amount of time value) instead of 100% of spot to actually hold BTC, freeing up capital while still moving roughly delta-for-delta with the underlying. Selling the near-dated call collects income against it, just like a covered call.

Max profit: reached exactly at the short strike at front expiry (the long leg still has some remaining time value there, so it's usually a bit more than the naive "strike width minus debit" estimate). Max loss: capped at the net debit paid — a real advantage over an actual covered call, whose downside is only bounded by BTC reaching zero.

Key risk: the long call's remaining value at front expiry is estimated via Black-Scholes at its current IV — a simplification, since IV can move by then — and the long call isn't a perfect substitute for spot: it still has some time value and delta below 1.0, so it won't track price exactly.

Front leg settles at intrinsic; the long-dated back leg is repriced via Black-Scholes at its current IV, since it hasn't expired yet

Not financial advice. "Far expiry" here means the furthest expiry Deribit currently lists, not an actual multi-year LEAPS contract — crypto options don't trade that far out. Capital efficiency is a real advantage (much less paid up front than buying BTC outright), but the long call still expires eventually and needs to be rolled, unlike BTC itself.
